\begin{abstract}
High-resolution surface weather fields are essential for regional climate applications,
yet global models operate at resolutions far too coarse to capture the fine-scale
variability that matters for floods, heat waves, and wind energy.
Generative diffusion models have recently shown promise for closing this gap,
but their computational cost grows sharply with spatial resolution,
limiting practical use.
We introduce \textbf{Latent CorrDiff}, a downscaling system that combines a
deterministic regression baseline with an ensemble generator that operates
entirely in a compressed latent space.
Applied to ERA5 reanalysis at \textasciitilde27\,km resolution, the system produces
physically consistent 4\,km surface fields over the continental United States across
six simultaneous output variables.
Because the diffusion process operates at one-sixteenth the spatial resolution of
the output, inference is \textbf{6.8$\times$ faster} and uses \textbf{5$\times$ less memory}
than an equivalent pixel-space approach, while preserving fine-scale spectral structure.
Each ensemble member is generated by adding a stochastically sampled residual
to the deterministic prediction, so the deterministic baseline is always recoverable
as the ensemble mean --- a property that simplifies evaluation and makes probabilistic
skill directly attributable to the diffusion stage.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Weather and climate impacts play out at the scale of valleys, coastlines, and cities
--- scales that global models cannot resolve.
Downscaling is the practice of translating coarse model output into fine-resolution
fields, and it has been central to regional climate science for decades.
Classical statistical methods learn simple corrections from historical data:
bias adjustment, quantile mapping, spatial interpolation.
These are fast and interpretable, but they produce single deterministic predictions
and cannot represent genuine uncertainty in the high-frequency spatial structure
that the coarse input does not contain.

Neural networks have substantially improved on classical methods.
Convolutional architectures recover fine-scale texture and correct systematic biases
with low average error.
But they share the same fundamental limitation: a single output per input.
In practice, a given synoptic pattern is consistent with a range of fine-scale
realizations --- different convective cell placements, different mountain wave
patterns, different precipitation footprints.
A deterministic prediction ignores this spread, which is precisely the information
most valuable for probabilistic risk assessment.

Diffusion models offer a principled way to sample from the full conditional
distribution rather than just its mean.
Recent work has demonstrated that diffusion-based downscaling produces sharp,
physically plausible outputs that match observed spatial statistics.
\citet{mardani2025corrdiff} improved on na\"ive pixel-space diffusion by factoring
the problem into a deterministic regression stage followed by a residual diffusion
stage --- the network only models the part of the output the regression could
not explain.
\citet{lopezgomez2025r2d2} extended probabilistic downscaling across lead times
and multiple climate models, showing the approach generalizes beyond a single
training distribution.

Despite this progress, two practical obstacles remain.
First, pixel-space diffusion at high resolution is expensive: each denoising step
requires a full-resolution network forward pass, and producing calibrated ensembles
requires many steps and many members.
At $256\times256$ pixels, this already strains GPU memory; at operational
kilometer-scale resolutions it becomes prohibitive.
Second, existing systems are typically evaluated on single variables or small
subsets independently, leaving open how well they capture the \emph{joint}
distribution across variables --- which matters whenever downstream applications
use multiple fields simultaneously.

This paper addresses both obstacles.
We describe a system that applies diffusion in the compressed representation of a
variational autoencoder rather than in pixel space, reducing the effective
spatial resolution of the diffusion process by a factor of four in each dimension.
We evaluate on six surface variables simultaneously and show that the latent approach
recovers the spectral characteristics of the target fields while running substantially
faster than a pixel-space baseline.

\section{Approach}

\subsection{Problem Setup}

The input is a snapshot of ERA5 reanalysis --- six meteorological surface variables
on a regular latitude-longitude grid at roughly 0.25$^\circ$ resolution --- together
with six static geographic fields describing the terrain over the continental
United States (terrain height, orographic variance, latitude, longitude, leaf area
index, and a land-sea mask).
The target output is a corresponding snapshot of the CONUS404 regional reanalysis
at 4\,km resolution, providing six surface variables: 2-m temperature (T2),
2-m dewpoint (TD2), 10-m zonal wind (U10), 10-m meridional wind (V10),
surface pressure (PSFC), and non-convective precipitation (PREC\_ACC\_NC).

The core challenge is that this mapping is one-to-many: the same large-scale
atmospheric state is compatible with many fine-scale realizations.
We want to sample from the distribution of plausible fine-scale fields given the
coarse input, not merely predict the conditional mean.

\subsection{Three-Stage Design}

Rather than train a single network end-to-end, we decompose the problem into three
stages that each handle a distinct aspect of the difficulty.

\paragraph{Stage 1 --- Deterministic regression.}
A U-Net regression network (DRN, 49.6M parameters) maps the 12-channel coarse input
directly to a fine-scale prediction at full $256\times256$ resolution.
This stage handles the bulk of the predictable structure:
large-scale temperature gradients, terrain-forced flows, systematic
humidity-elevation relationships.
Its output is deterministic and represents the conditional mean of the target
given the input.

\paragraph{Stage 2 --- Residual compression.}
A variational autoencoder (VAE, 39.7M parameters) compresses the \emph{residual}
between the target and the DRN prediction into an 8-channel latent map
at $64\times64$ spatial resolution --- a 16$\times$ reduction in spatial tokens.
The residual encodes fine-scale variability that the regression could not explain:
the stochastic component of the downscaling problem.
At inference, new residuals are drawn by sampling the latent prior rather than
encoding a specific target.

\paragraph{Stage 3 --- Latent diffusion.}
A diffusion U-Net (142.4M parameters) generates samples in the compressed
latent space, conditioned on the original coarse ERA5 input.
The 30-channel conditioning context is formed by concatenating:
the noisy latent sample (8 channels), ERA5 bilinearly resampled to latent
resolution (12 channels), the VAE posterior mean from the DRN prediction
(8 channels), and on-the-fly positional coordinate embeddings (2 channels).
Each generated latent is decoded by the VAE and added to the DRN output,
yielding a full-resolution ensemble member.

\subsection{Why Latent Space?}

The efficiency argument is straightforward.
At $256\times256$ output resolution, each pixel-space denoising step touches
every output pixel.
Operating in the $64\times64$ latent space reduces the spatial token count by $16\times$,
giving a commensurate reduction in the cost of each network pass.
Empirically, 32 denoising steps at latent resolution takes 2.13\,s on an A100 GPU,
versus 14.5\,s for an equivalent pixel-space U-Net --- a \textbf{6.8$\times$ speedup}
--- while peak GPU memory drops from 11.0\,GB to 2.19\,GB
(Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}).
This makes ensemble generation with 32 members computationally comparable to a
single pixel-space run.

\subsection{Training}

All three stages are trained independently on CONUS404 reanalysis data from
1980--2010, with 2011--2020 held out for evaluation.
Patches of $256\times256$ pixels are randomly sampled subject to a minimum
land-fraction constraint to avoid ocean-dominated regions.
All variables are z-score normalized using training-set statistics;
precipitation receives a $\log(1+x)$ transform before normalization to compress
its heavy tail.
The DRN is trained with a combination of L1 and L2 loss.
The VAE is trained with reconstruction, KL divergence, and perceptual losses.
The diffusion model follows the EDM noise schedule~\citep{karras2022edm}
with Heun's second-order ODE sampler and an exponential moving average
of weights used at inference.
Classifier-free guidance~\citep{ho2022cfg} with scale 0.2 is applied during sampling.

the CONUS404 target across all six output variables.
The DRN output is spectrally damped relative to the target at high wavenumbers,

Adding the diffusion residual recovers spectral energy at the scales the DRN
suppresses, matching the target slope through the inertial subrange.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}

The current system treats each time step independently and does not enforce
temporal consistency across a sequence of outputs.
This is acceptable for snapshot-based evaluation but limits applicability
to time-series diagnostics such as diurnal cycles or storm tracks.
The model is trained and evaluated on a single reanalysis product (CONUS404)
and a single input (ERA5); generalization to GCM projections or other
reanalyses is not demonstrated.
Finally, evaluation here is limited to spatial metrics and spectral statistics;
full probabilistic calibration (CRPS, rank histograms, spread-skill ratio)
across the complete test set is left for follow-on work.

\section{Related Work}

Statistical downscaling has a long history in climate science.
Early neural approaches \citep{maraun2018stat} focused on single-site
corrections and were later extended to spatial fields.
\citet{leinonen2020stochastic} applied generative adversarial networks to
precipitation disaggregation.
Score-based and diffusion models entered the field via \citet{price2023gencast}
for global forecasting, then \citet{mardani2025corrdiff} for regional
downscaling with the CorrDiff residual decomposition that directly motivates
our work.
The latent diffusion idea originates with \citet{rombach2022ldm} in the
image synthesis literature; we adapt it to the geophysical setting where
the latent encodes a physical residual rather than a compressed image.
Concurrent work \citet{lopezgomez2025r2d2} explores generalization across
climate models; we focus on the efficiency axis left largely unexamined in
that line of work.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented Latent CorrDiff, a three-stage probabilistic downscaling system
that performs diffusion in a compressed latent space rather than in pixel space.
The approach achieves a 6.8$\times$ inference speedup and 5$\times$ memory reduction
over a pixel-space baseline while recovering the spectral structure of the target
fields.
Operating simultaneously over six surface variables with a shared model makes
the joint output distribution physically consistent in a way that per-variable
pipelines are not.
The residual design ensures the deterministic baseline remains a special case,
enabling clean attribution of probabilistic skill to the generative stage.
